# B Active — Pricing Strategy
### A plain-English guide for setting (and holding) your prices
Version 1.0 · 31 May 2026 · Prepared for The Perry Group

> **Read this alongside** `B_Active_Product_&_Pricing_Master.xlsx` (the numbers) and the `B Active Build Kit/research/07_ph_pricing_benchmarks.md` report (the evidence). This document explains the *thinking* so you can make confident pricing decisions yourselves.
>
> **A quick caveat:** these are well-researched recommendations, not financial advice or guarantees. The figures use your real costs plus reasonable assumptions about fees, shipping and returns. Revisit them once you have a few months of real sales data.

---

## 1. The one idea to hold onto

**You are a premium-accessible brand. Price on the value you deliver and where the market sits — not just on what it cost you to make, and never by being the cheapest.**

The fastest way to destroy a premium brand is to price (and discount) like a cheap one. A low price doesn't just earn less money — it *tells the customer the product is low quality* before she's even touched it. Your job is to sit confidently **below the imports** (Lululemon, Nike, Alo at PH prices) and **clearly above the mass-market** (Decathlon, Shopee/Lazada generic, even Bench's pickleball line). That gap is your home.

What that looks like in numbers (per garment, in ₱):

| Tier | Dress | Skort | Leggings | Bra | Where you want to be |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Imports (Nike/Lulu PH) | 3,495–5,795 | 2,095–3,095 | 1,695–7,360 | 1,295–4,400 | *below this* |
| **B Active (recommended)** | **1,890–2,650** | **990–1,290** | **1,090–1,190** | **950** | **← here** |

## 2. How we set your prices (the method)

We used a **cost-up, market-anchored** method — the approach serious brands use:

1. **Start with the true cost** of each item (not the optimistic "profit" in your sourcing sheet — more on that below).
2. **Look at what the market charges** for the same category in the Philippines, by tier.
3. **Place each product** at the point that (a) protects a healthy margin, (b) lands in the premium-accessible gap, and (c) hits a price the Filipina shopper reads as "considered," not cheap.

### The "true cost" wake-up call
Your `Barry Active.xlsx` sheet shows a "profit" column — but that profit only subtracts the **product cost**. It quietly ignores everything else it takes to land a sale. Real life adds several more layers:

```
   Product cost (what the factory charges)              ← the only thing the sheet counted
 + Inbound: branding (logo, hangtags) + freight to you  ≈ ₱150 / item
 + Packaging (mailer, tissue, thank-you card)           ≈ ₱25 / item
 ─────────────────────────────────────────────
 = FULLY-LOADED COST  (what the item really costs you landed)
 + Payment processing (GCash/card fees)                 ≈ 2.5% of price
 + Outbound shipping you absorb (free-ship, subsidy)    ≈ ₱90 / order
 + Returns / COD bounce allowance                       ≈ ₱120 / order
 ─────────────────────────────────────────────
 = what's left = NET CONTRIBUTION  (before marketing & overhead)
```

This is why a skort that "looks" like ₱400 profit is really closer to ₱180 once everything is counted. **Knowing your true number is the whole game** — it's the difference between a brand that grows and one that quietly loses money on every "profitable" sale.

## 4. Why we *raised* your cheapest items (this is the important part)

Your dresses and sets were priced sensibly. But your **skorts, leggings, tank and bra were priced like mass-market product** — ₱595 to ₱895. Three problems with that:

1. **It barely made money.** At ₱595, the ribbed tank actually *lost* a peso or two per sale once shipping and fees were counted. The bra and skorts were only a little better.
2. **It contradicted the brand.** A shopper who sees a ₱595 tank next to a "premium pickleball" story gets a mixed message. Price is the loudest quality signal you have. Premium brands don't sell ₱595 tanks.
3. **It left easy money on the table.** Moving the tank to ₱895 and skorts to ₱990–1,290 keeps you *far* below imports and still competitive with Bench — but turns thin or negative items into real contributors.

Raising entry prices on a premium brand is almost always right. The customer who wants your brand is not the customer hunting the ₱299 Shopee skort — and trying to win her with a low price just trains your *real* customer to expect cheap.

## 5. Gross margin vs. net contribution (in plain terms)

Two numbers matter, and people confuse them:

- **Gross margin** = (price − fully-loaded cost) ÷ price. It's the "headline" profit on the product itself. Yours average **~43%**. Healthy for apparel made in small batches.
- **Net contribution** = what's left *after* the costs of actually selling and delivering it (payment fees, shipping you absorb, returns/COD bounce). Yours average **~26%**, or about **₱500 per item**. This is the money that pays for marketing, your time, the store, and profit.

Then there's the layer we did **not** bake into each item: **marketing (customer acquisition)**. In the PH market, winning a *new* customer can cost ₱800–₱2,500. That means **your first sale to a new customer is often near break-even or a small loss — and you make your money on the second, third and fourth orders.** This is normal for DTC brands. It's why two things matter enormously:

- **Average order value (AOV):** get her to buy a dress *and* a skort, not just one item.
- **Lifetime value (LTV):** get her to come back. Email, community, quality, and fit (your 7-day exchange) are what bring her back.

## 6. Think in a portfolio, not item-by-item

Not every product needs the same margin. Use each for a job:

- **Traffic-drivers / entry (skorts, tank, bra — ₱795–1,290):** lower margin, but they get a first-time, price-curious shopper to try the brand and lift AOV. They don't have to be your best earners.
- **Profit-drivers (dresses & the set — ₱1,890–2,650):** these earn ₱740–935 net each. This is where the brand makes its money. Merchandise them front and centre (The Court Edit).
- **Bundles:** a "Court-Ready Set" (dress + matching tank or skort, or "buy the look") at a small, *built-in* saving lifts AOV without ever running a "sale." E.g. *Dress + Skort bundle ₱2,790* (vs ₱3,080 separately) — frames value, protects price integrity.

## 7. Pricing psychology for the Filipina shopper

The PH market has clear mental price "walls." Work *with* them:

- **₱1,000 wall:** the first hesitation point. Entry items sit just under it (₱990) or just over with clear premium cues (₱1,090–1,290).
- **₱2,000 wall:** "this is an investment piece" territory. Keep core dresses just under (₱1,890–1,990); let only the genuinely premium dresses cross it (₱2,190–2,650), where they still look like a *bargain* next to ₱3,495+ imports.
- **₱3,000 wall:** "imported brand" territory — avoid for core apparel.
- **Price endings:** use ₱X90 / ₱X95 / ₱X,450 (e.g. 990, 1,290, 2,450). These read as *considered*. Avoid ₱X99 spam-y endings and avoid round ₱1,000/₱2,000 (feels arbitrary).
- **Anchoring:** always show a premium dress (₱2,650) near the entry skort (₱990). The expensive item makes the cheaper one feel like great value — and vice-versa, the entry item makes the brand feel accessible.

## 8. Free shipping is a pricing lever

Set **free shipping at ₱2,000**. Here's the logic: your average dress is ~₱2,000, and your entry items are ₱800–1,300. The threshold gently pushes a one-skort shopper (₱990) to add a tank (₱895) and cross ₱2,000 — lifting AOV by more than the shipping costs you. Show a "**You're ₱X away from free shipping**" bar in the cart. It's one of the highest-ROI nudges in e-commerce, and it never cheapens the brand.

---

## 9. Payments & COD — protect your margin

- **GCash/Maya/cards via PayMongo** cost ~2.5%. Fine — most shoppers expect them.
- **Cash on Delivery (COD)** is loved in the Philippines but **20–40% of COD orders bounce** (customer not home / changes mind), and you eat the round-trip shipping. So:
  - Add a small **COD handling fee** (₱50) and consider capping COD to orders under ₱2,500.
  - **Confirm COD orders** by SMS/Viber before you ship.
  - **Gently steer to prepaid** — e.g. "Pay with GCash and skip the COD fee." Every prepaid order is a more profitable, lower-risk order.

## 10. Promotions: what to do instead of discounting

**Hold the line on price.** Once customers learn to wait for your "sale," your full price is dead and your margin with it. The brands you admire (Lululemon, Alo, Vuori) almost never discount core product. Neither should you. Instead:

- **A welcome offer** (5% off the first order for email signups) — private, one-time, builds your list. *This is the one standing discount.*
- **Bundles** ("buy the look") — value without a markdown.
- **Free shipping threshold** — feels like a win, protects price.
- **Gift-with-purchase** (a branded scrunchie, a paddle cover) — adds value, not price cuts.
- **"Editions," not "sales"** — when you want to move stock, frame it as a limited colour or a seasonal "Court Edit" drop, not a clearance. If you must clear end-of-line, do it quietly (a single "Last Sets" page), never a sitewide banner.
- **Never** use countdown timers, "X sold today," or sitewide % off. (See the DO/DON'T table in the build guide.)

## 11. When and how to change prices

- **Review quarterly** once you're live, using real data: which items sell, your true returns/COD rate, your actual ad cost per sale.
- **Raising prices** is normal and fine — do it on *new* arrivals first (customers anchor to the new price), and on existing items when costs/FX rise. Small, occasional increases on a growing brand are expected.
- **Introductory pricing:** you can launch a brand-new style slightly low for the first few weeks framed as "introducing," then settle to its real price — but use sparingly.
- **Watch your costs:** your costs are driven by **order size and FX**. Larger production runs cut the per-unit branding/freight (~₱150 now) significantly — which either widens your margin or lets you hold price while competitors raise theirs. Track the USD→PHP rate (your costs are in USD).

## 12. How to price a new product (reusable worksheet)

When you add any new style, run these five steps (the spreadsheet's Pricing Model does the maths — just add a row):

1. **Fully-loaded cost** = factory cost + ₱150 (branding/freight) + ₱25 (packaging).
2. **Target price** = fully-loaded cost ÷ (1 − target margin). For ~45% gross, that's *cost × 1.8*; for ~60%, *cost × 2.5*.
3. **Sanity-check against the market** (Section 1 table): are you comfortably below imports and above mass? If your target lands too high for the category, either accept a lower margin or rethink the product cost.
4. **Round to a PH-friendly ending** that respects the nearest price wall (₱990, ₱1,290, ₱1,890, ₱2,450…).
5. **Check net contribution** isn't thin: price − fully-loaded cost − (2.5% × price) − ₱90 − ₱120 should leave a comfortable cushion (aim ₱200+ on entry items, ₱600+ on dresses).

---

## 13. Cheat sheet

- Sit **below imports, above mass-market.** That gap is the brand.
- **Know your true (loaded) cost** — the sourcing sheet's "profit" is optimistic.
- **Don't price entry items cheap** — it loses money and weakens the brand.
- **Gross ~43%, net ~26%** is your current shape; protect it.
- **You make money on repeat orders**, so chase AOV and LTV, not just the first sale.
- **Free shipping at ₱2,000** to lift baskets.
- **Steer to prepaid**, fee + confirm COD.
- **Never discount core product.** Use bundles, welcome offer, free-ship, editions.
- **Use PH price endings** (₱X90/₱X,450) and respect the ₱1,000 / ₱2,000 / ₱3,000 walls.
- **Review quarterly** with real numbers; raise prices on new arrivals first.
